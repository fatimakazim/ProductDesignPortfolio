Role & Contribution

I designed and built this experience end to end: the concept, the visitor journey, the narrative structure, the 3D reconstruction, the interaction design, the narrator, the audio system, and the implementation — and I was also responsible for taking it from a browser build to a working installation at NYU Shanghai's capstone exhibition, a three-day show where it ran for walk-up visitors rather than living only as a link on a server.

That combination mattered. Because I could move directly between a design idea and a working prototype, I could test whether a concept held up the moment I had it. And because I knew it had to work in front of strangers on a show floor, not just for me at my desk, I designed every interaction assuming the visitor had never touched VR before and had about ten seconds of patience before deciding whether to stay.

The Experience Challenge

Most presentations of Partition history rely on text, photographs, or timelines — formats that describe events from the outside. They're useful for information, but they keep the reader at a distance from the thing they're trying to understand: a lived, physical, embodied loss.

The question I set out to answer was simple to state and hard to solve: how might I turn historical memory from something people read about into something they can move through? And because the answer needed to work as a live installation, not just a demo I narrated myself, it came with a second question: how do I make that work for a visitor who walks up to a booth, has never worn a headset, and has to understand what to do within seconds?

That's an experience-design problem before it's a technical one. It meant deciding what a visitor should feel oriented by, what should invite curiosity, what should be optional, and where the story needed to interrupt free exploration to make sure a specific memory landed — all while assuming zero prior instruction. The 3D reconstruction, the narrator, and the audio system came after those decisions; they're the medium, not the point.

Experience Concept

The experience is built around a single real place: Saidpur Village, on the outskirts of Islamabad. Its architecture still carries the layers of the communities who lived there before Partition — temples, a mosque, residential streets, and market lanes sit within walking distance of each other, evidence of a multicultural life that Partition interrupted.

Grounding the experience in one specific village, instead of a generalized or composite setting, was a deliberate choice. It meant the story could stay concrete rather than symbolic, and it meant the environment itself could do narrative work: a temple standing next to a mosque tells a visitor something a caption never could.

That's why the architecture isn't a backdrop for the story — it's the interface. Oral testimony is attached to the specific locations where those memories happened, so a visitor doesn't consume the story in a fixed order. They uncover it the way memory itself works: unevenly, tied to place, and shaped by where curiosity leads them.

Visitor Journey

Underneath the 3D environment and the narrator system is a journey I designed stage by stage, thinking about attention, orientation, and pacing before I thought about implementation — and thinking about it for a stranger stepping up to a headset at a show, not for a visitor who already knew what to expect.

  • A

    Entry — Cinematic Threshold

    A brief holographic sequence sets historical context before the visitor takes control. It exists to give first-time visitors a reason to care before they're handed navigation — and it's skippable, so it never gates anyone who's already been queuing to try it.

  • B

    Orientation

    The narrator appears and establishes itself as a guide rather than a character to follow blindly. This is the moment a visitor who's never used VR learns the basic grammar of the space: how to move, and that the polyhedron is safe to approach.

  • C

    Exploring the Reconstructed Village

    Visitors move freely through Saidpur, or let the narrator lead them between ten historically significant locations. Either way, agency stays with the visitor — guidance is offered, not imposed, which matters when you can't predict how comfortable a given visitor will be with the controls.

  • D

    Discovering Hotspots

    Interactive markers surface naturally as visitors approach specific buildings and streets, rewarding exploration rather than demanding it — a deliberately low-effort way to keep someone curious a few seconds longer.

  • E

    Entering 360° Documentary Media

    Selecting a hotspot transitions the visitor into a panoramic photo or video capture of the real location — a shift from reconstruction to document that grounds the story in something that actually exists.

  • F

    Hearing Oral Testimony

    Spatial audio delivers a resident's memory of that exact location, timed so the visitor is looking at the place being described while they hear about it.

  • G

    Returning to the Reconstruction

    The visitor is brought back into the 3D village rather than left in the documentary layer, so the reconstruction keeps functioning as the spine of the experience rather than a menu.

  • H

    Continuing to the Next Location

    The narrator resumes its role and the cycle of movement, discovery, and testimony repeats — with enough variation in pacing that it holds up over a few minutes at a booth, not just a single scripted pass.

Experience Architecture

The journey above is built on three interconnected layers. Each gives a visitor a different way of relating to the same physical place, and moving between them is itself part of the design — reconstruction, documentary media, and testimony are meant to feel like different depths of the same world, not separate apps stitched together.

1. Cinematic Threshold

Purpose: give the visitor a reason to care before asking them to explore. What the visitor does: watches a short holographic sequence, or skips it immediately. Why I designed it this way: a cold open into free exploration works for a visitor who already knows the history, but loses everyone else in the first ten seconds of a booth interaction; a mandatory introduction loses the opposite group, who won't wait through it in a public setting. Making it skippable resolved that without picking a side.

2. Interactive Architectural Reconstruction

Purpose: function as the "home base" of the experience — the space a visitor always returns to. What the visitor does: walks or teleports through a GLTF reconstruction of the village, either freely or along a guided path through ten locations. Why I designed it this way: free exploration gives a visitor ownership of the experience; the guided path exists for anyone who'd rather be shown around than figure it out themselves — which, at a show, is most first-time visitors. Both routes lead to the same hotspots, so neither visitor sees a lesser version of the village.

3. Immersive 360° Documentary Experiences

Purpose: connect the reconstruction back to the real, physical village. What the visitor does: steps into panoramic photo and video captures of the actual site, layered with spatial audio recorded from local residents. Why I designed it this way: a fully reconstructed environment risks feeling like a diorama. Cutting to real photographic and video documentation at the moment of testimony makes it unmistakable that this happened somewhere real, before returning the visitor to the model to continue.

The Polyhedron Narrator

I deliberately avoided a human avatar as the guide. A face or a body implies a single storyteller, and these are collective memories that belong to many residents, not one. Instead, the narrator is an abstract holographic polyhedron, its form inspired by the geometric solid in Dürer's Melencolia I — recognizable as a presence, but not a person.

Its job is continuity. Every stop in the journey looks and sounds different — reconstruction, documentary photo, testimony audio — and the narrator is the one constant across all of them, so a visitor never loses the thread of who's guiding them or why. It responds visually to how close a visitor stands, which gives it enough presence to feel attentive without needing dialogue or a face to do that work.

Guided navigation matters here specifically because this is a ten-stop journey, run at a public exhibition for visitors who mostly hadn't used VR before: without an option to be led, a visitor unfamiliar with 3D navigation could easily miss half the village or wander off entirely. The narrator makes that option feel like being shown around by a companion rather than being put on rails.

Behind the interaction

  • State Machine A custom behavioural system governs the narrator through states for movement, waiting, narration, arrival, and completion.
  • Reactive Behaviour Glow intensity, particle density, and rotation adjust dynamically based on the visitor's proximity.
  • Custom GLSL Shaders The holographic appearance is handwritten in GLSL, layered with particle systems and orbital rings.
  • Guided Navigation Smooth interpolation lets the narrator travel naturally between the ten locations across the reconstructed village.

Designing for the Show Floor

Running this as a live installation at a three-day exhibition — rather than as a link people opened on their own time — changed what "usability" meant. A visitor at a booth has no manual, no onboarding call, and usually no patience for either. They either understand what to do in the first few seconds or they walk away.

That shaped concrete decisions. The experience runs on desktop and in VR from the same build, so it could flex to whatever setup the booth had on a given day. The introduction is skippable, guided and free exploration are both always available, and every hotspot is visually distinct enough that a first-time visitor never has to wonder where they're allowed to interact — there was no one standing behind them to explain it every time.

None of this lowers the ambition of the experience. It's the opposite: immersive technology that requires a visitor to fight it stops being immersive the moment they notice it. Designing for people encountering the piece cold, in a public space, for the first and only time, is what made it a real installation rather than a demo that only worked when I was the one explaining it.

Turning Content Into Experience

Every piece of testimony is anchored to the exact location where it happened, rather than collected into a transcript a visitor scrolls through. That single decision changes what the content is doing: it's not being displayed to the visitor, it's being discovered by them, at the pace and in the order they choose to move.

The stories themselves describe neighbourhoods, courtyards, markets, friendships, and daily routines rather than political events in the abstract. Paired with spatial audio, hearing them while standing in the reconstructed courtyard or street they describe gives the words a sense of place that reading them never would.

Architecture, photography, video, and audio are all doing narrative work here, not just decoration. That's the core content-design idea behind the whole project: memory accumulates through movement, not through more text.

Technical Stack

Built entirely for the browser, so the experience could run on the installation's desktop setup, on mobile, or on a VR headset without installing a native application — each technical choice below was made for what it let the experience do, not for its own sake.

  • A-Frame / WebXR → one build that runs across desktop and headset without separate versions.
  • Three.js → direct control over rendering where A-Frame's defaults weren't enough, especially for the narrator.
  • GLTF 3D Environment → the reconstructed village, optimized to load and run in real time in a browser.
  • Web Audio API → reliable spatial, narrative audio that keeps playing throughout a WebXR session.
  • GLSL Shaders → the narrator's holographic look, built by hand rather than from a stock material.
  • Raycaster Interaction → one hotspot system that works with a mouse cursor or a VR controller.
  • VR + Desktop Controls → the same journey stays accessible regardless of what hardware a visitor has, or what the booth had that day.
  • JavaScript → custom A-Frame components tying the narrator, hotspots, and audio system together.

Technical Challenge — Solving WebXR Audio

  • 01

    Browser Audio Restrictions

    Problem: narration is the backbone of the guided journey — if it fails mid-session, a visitor at a live installation has no way of knowing whether that's intentional or broken.

    Constraint: browsers frequently block playback from a standard HTMLAudioElement once an active WebXR session begins, even after the visitor has already interacted with the page. Narrated VR experiences built the conventional way go silent without warning the moment a headset session starts — a serious risk on a show floor, where nobody's watching to notice and restart it.

    My approach: I built a custom NarrationAudioManager on the Web Audio API. Narration clips preload into AudioBuffers, get cached, and play through AudioBufferSourceNodes routed into a single audio pipeline.

    Result: after one user gesture unlocks the AudioContext, narration plays reliably for the rest of the session — something that mattered every single time a new visitor put the headset on over the course of the exhibition.

Design Principles

  • 01

    Story Before Technology.

    Every technical decision — shaders, locomotion, audio architecture — was chosen to serve a moment in the story, not the other way around.

  • 02

    Memory Is Spatial.

    Stories land harder when they're tied to a specific place a visitor can stand in, rather than presented as disconnected facts.

  • 03

    Visitor Agency.

    Free exploration and guided navigation are both always available — the visitor decides how much to be led.

  • 04

    Design for the First-Time Visitor.

    Every onboarding step and cinematic can be skipped, and nothing assumes the visitor has used VR before or has anyone nearby to explain it.

  • 05

    Multiple Perspectives.

    The same location is presented as architecture, documentary media, and personal testimony at once, so visitors build their own understanding rather than receive a single account.

What This Project Demonstrates

This wasn't a commercial client project — there was no brand, venue contract, or business outcome attached to it. It was designed, built, and exhibited as a real installation at NYU Shanghai's capstone show, a three-day event where it ran for the public rather than living only as a link I sent people. That distinction matters: the experience had to hold up in front of visitors I didn't know, without me there to explain it every time.

  • Designing around a guest, not a screen Every decision started from what a visitor walking up cold would notice, feel, or do next.
  • Structuring a complete journey Entry, orientation, exploration, and return were all designed deliberately for someone with no prior context.
  • Building for a physical installation The experience had to run reliably across a three-day public exhibition, not just in a controlled demo environment.
  • Working across touchpoints 3D reconstruction, documentary media, spatial audio, and interaction design had to function as one coherent experience, not separate features.
  • Prototyping ideas directly Because I could build what I designed, concepts got tested against a real, running installation rather than a deck.

I'd approach a commercial installation the same way: understand what the guest is meant to feel and do at each stage first, design for someone encountering it with zero instruction, and only then decide which technologies earn their place in delivering that.

Reflection

This project changed how I think about immersive technology — not as a visual upgrade over a screen, but as a way of letting content live in space, where movement and attention become part of the storytelling itself.

Watching people encounter it cold over three days at the exhibition, rather than only testing it myself, taught me how different "working" is from "working for a stranger who's never done this before." Small things — how obvious a hotspot needed to look, how long an intro could run before people lost patience — only became clear once real visitors were the ones standing there.

If I were taking this experience toward a larger, ongoing public installation, the next steps would build on that: more structured observation of where visitors hesitate or disengage, understanding throughput for multiple simultaneous guests, and thinking through how the piece could integrate with a permanent physical space rather than a temporary booth.
